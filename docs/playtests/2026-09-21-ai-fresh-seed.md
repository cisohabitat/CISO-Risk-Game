## Comparison with annual review

The broad prediction held. Cloud completion, unfinished recovery, staff fatigue, and business trade-offs drove the verdict. The review credited formal command in **both** incidents, consistent with the decisions actually recorded. The later ransomware incident and three failed commercial objectives made the final year worse than the Q3 picture.

The review supplied important information unavailable in the incident messages:

- Customer exposure: Partner Portal → Identity Platform → Order Management System.
- Ransomware: Corvus Managed Services → Remote Access → Identity Platform → Management Jump Servers → Production Cloud.
- MFA/PAM exceptions and incomplete telemetry hurt both routes. Cloud posture, prompt detection, and formal command helped ransomware response.

My real omissions were substantial. I did not follow the repeatedly surfaced identity-concentration issue into a formal assessment or fund identity uplift. I commissioned only three enquiries, independently assessed only two controls, and left many dependencies unexamined. I allowed programme fatigue to persist and spent too much too early. These are player mistakes, not grounds to dismiss the mixed result.

However, several explanations were imprecise or difficult to reconcile:

1. **“Nothing brought [identity concentration] into view” is inaccurate as player-facing feedback.** The briefing explicitly displayed the shared-identity-platform pattern repeatedly, including March, April, and May. I saw it but did not pursue it. The review should distinguish surfaced, investigated, and formally raised.
2. **“The shape of the organisation itself went unexamined” overstates the omission.** I began with architecture and commissioned the dependency review. The day-96 follow-up specifically credited my grasp of the organisation’s shape. A narrower claim about incomplete dependency verification would fit the run.
3. **Retention harm is unexplained after enforcement.** I chose Enforce the retention policy on day 103, yet excess retention appears under what hurt the day-216 exposure. This might represent incomplete implementation or historic exposure, but neither was explained during play. It is a narrative inconsistency requiring investigation, not proof of a simulation bug.
4. **Recovery wording mixes levels.** The ransomware path says workloads and backups were encrypted together, while what helped includes backups unreachable from compromised accounts. Mixed backup populations could explain this, but the review does not distinguish them. It also cites a rehearsed decision path while the production recovery runbook remained unproven. Incident coordination and service restoration need clearer separation.
5. **Sustainability summary omits its own central function.** It says incident response is spent, then lists SOC, Engineering, Architecture, Cyber risk, and Identity, without an incident-response line.

## Confusion, missed cues, and engagement

**Highest-impact interaction problem:** the large sticky incident panel covered target controls after automatic scrolling. At day 345, Decide and inbox interactions failed or appeared to do nothing. Screenshots showed the overlap. Manual positioning and clicking the visibly unobscured control worked. This was especially disruptive during an active incident.

**Weak incident feedback:** escalation, consequence, recovery, debrief, and closure messages were mostly generic one-line updates. The first incident resolved across one advance without a meaningful damage account. Attack reconstruction arrived only at year end. Give the player enough interim detail to make the post-incident learning decision meaningful.

**Unclear board feedback:** “too many items” was actionable; “one material item omitted” without naming it was not. The Q1 nothing-to-report state also arrived after substantial work because Emerging versus Open had not become salient.

**Capacity versus health:** available capacity and burning-out staff can coexist legitimately, but “Your functions have room to take on work” is too reassuring beside that fatigue warning. A clearer combined cue would have helped me avoid treating unused days as organisational health.

**Missed cues:** I relied heavily on Briefing and left much of Inbox unread. I did not trace identity concentration when prompted. I left recovery paused longer than intended while waiting for recruitment and then cloud completion. Recruitment still said Recruiting after capacity rose, making completion less obvious. These are observed player behaviours, not claims that the information was absent.

**Engagement:** the budget consequences, workload correction, warehouse fallback payoff, and commercial compromises were engaging. I did not experience a clear boredom point during the decision-heavy sections. Repeated generic incident updates and the large unread inbox encouraged skimming rather than curiosity. Long Skip ahead jumps made the late-year pacing feel passive.

## Suggested priorities

1. Prevent the incident panel from obscuring interactive controls.
2. Provide incident-specific route, impact, containment, and recovery facts before the annual review.
3. Name material board omissions and explain agenda constraints before submission.
4. Make review language distinguish what was surfaced, examined, formally raised, and treated.
5. Explain partial implementation when later incident findings appear to contradict earlier decisions.
