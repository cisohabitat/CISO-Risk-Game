# CISO: First Year, seed harbour-87524, an assistant-operated year

**An AI playthrough, not a human playtest.** An assistant played one CISO year
on the live site, through the interface only, with recording on, and filled
in the observation sheet from `docs/playtest-kit/`. It had read
`docs/PLAYTEST.md` and the earlier findings first, so it was not blind. None
of it counts toward Phase 0's human playtest gate (`docs/ROADMAP.md`).

## Verdict

The year held together. The Q3 account and the review told the same story,
and the review's main judgment, "You built the capability on people who
cannot do it again", matched what the player had said at Q3. The player's
strongest disagreement was real, and it was a content defect.

**The headline defect.** On day 275 the player chose "Carry it until next
year's plan" on *The platform has outgrown its recovery design*. That
decision says outright that a restore takes longer than the business can
stand. The option still recorded "Backups recover critical services within
business tolerance." as the player's assumption. A day later it reported the
assumption "Was never true", and the review counted it against two of the
player's reasons. The player had relied on no such thing. They had accepted,
knowingly, that it was false.

## Found and fixed

Each fix in the first three rows has a test in
`tests/engine/playtest-harbour-87524.test.ts` that fails when the fix is
reverted.

| Finding | Fix |
|---|---|
| Carrying the platform's recovery gap recorded the opposite of what was accepted | The option now records "The customer platform does not go down before its recovery is re-planned." That is the bet actually being made. It fails only if an incident takes the platform down while it is carried. Priya's "Biggest weekend yet, nothing broke" no longer arrives if the platform did break. |
| "You are holding a risk assessment the board has never seen: a risk", on 6 April, after a Q1 paper that covered every High risk | The escalation now requires at least one live risk that no filed paper has covered. The text that names the risk and the condition that sends it share one helper, `src/game/risk/unreported.ts`. |
| "Nothing you did went near" the non-production route, after the player's cloud configuration review exposed the production peering | An enquiry that traced a dependency between two of a risk's own systems now counts as going near that risk. Funding nothing for it still shows in the score. |
| The assumed-breach test "report is in", without saying what it found | The message now says what was found: production administration reached from a standard laptop in under two days. It also says where the finding is filed. The full test's message names the legacy firewall rules it found. |
| The HR provider "has answered", without the answer | The message now gives the answer: what was in the stolen system, what was not, and what is being done for staff. |
| The launch conditions "were honoured", without saying which | The message now names them: second-factor sign-in for administrators, two checkout findings fixed, and the third dated against a vendor release. |
| "Built, not yet verified by a restore" beside a programme milestone named "Restore tested outside production" | The line now reads "Built, but you have not checked recovery yourself since". The milestone is the programme's own test; the line is about the player's check. |

### At the end of Q3, before the review

Ask in this order, write the answers verbatim.

Captured day 273, 1 October, at 16:33:50 UTC, before opening the annual review or Your year. Reproduced unchanged from the contemporaneous record.

1. In three sentences, what has your year been about?
"My year has been about making the business survivable while taking away the standing access that could let one compromise spread everywhere. I funded recovery and identity first, used evidence and explicit uncertainty with the board, and made narrower decisions about suppliers, checkout and the warehouse rather than trying to buy every programme. The cost was a tired team and an almost empty budget, which left me unable to buy the safer acquisition connection or the assurance work I now want."

2. How is your team doing?
"They have capacity on paper, but they are not healthy. Identity is still burning out, engineering was burning out earlier, and the capability review confirmed sustained overload; one recruitment round and pausing identity earlier did not make that disappear. I do not read the 'committed' or 'available' label as recovery."

3. Which risk worries you most, and why that one?
"The build pipeline remains my biggest worry: it is still High, and trusted automation can carry a compromised engineer's credentials into production affecting both payments and the customer platform. Identity is only 90% complete, and I have not funded the cloud programme, so the path is not demonstrably closed. Recovery being built but not independently verified is my next concern because I cannot afford the £80k restore enquiry."

### After the review

Captured after reading the complete annual review, around 16:39 UTC, before Stop and export. Reproduced unchanged from the assistant-player's recorded answers.

1. Which sentences in the review did you disagree with? (Point at them.)
"I disagree with the assumption count '1 stopped holding' and the reasoning verdict under 'Resource constraints require deferral': 'On 1 of them the assurance underneath it turned out not to hold.' I knowingly carried a platform that the decision already said could not restore within tolerance, because the redesign was unaffordable; I did not rely on assurance that it was within tolerance. The same mistaken assumption contaminates the executive-ownership reasoning verdict. I also find 'Non-production environment as a route into production was among the largest risks you inherited, and nothing you did went near it' too broad: I commissioned a cloud configuration review that exposed production peering, although I agree I did not fund the treatment programme. The main judgment, 'You built the capability on people who cannot do it again,' feels earned and matches my Q3 account."

2. What do you think you learned, if anything?
"Buying capability, getting it adopted and verifying that it works are different jobs. I ran out of money for proportionate late choices because I spent too much early, and keeping a programme fully staffed was not the same as keeping its people healthy. The failed production restore was a much better lesson than a percentage-complete bar."

3. Was there a moment you stopped understanding what the game wanted from you?
"The unnamed 'a risk' escalation on 6 April gave me no subject to judge, and several follow-ups said an answer or report had arrived without telling me what it contained. I also could not reconcile the game's invented within-tolerance assumption with the knowingly out-of-tolerance decision I had just made. I generally understood the main loop, though hiring-in-progress feedback and the distinction between a completed recovery milestone and an independently verified production restore took extra checking."

4. Was there a stretch you clicked through rather than played? When?
"From January I selectively skipped unread routine inbox items while following the Briefing's decisions and returned work; that was triage rather than boredom. By 19 August, day230, I was skimming the repeated risk descriptions and waiting for something that changed the decision, and the summer's long jumps felt flatter. Q4 became engaging again: enforcement, the production restore, supplier contract terms and the growth-related recovery gap felt like consequences of my own year."

5. Which difficulty did you pick, and did the name match what you got?
"CISO, because it was labelled the intended experience. The name fit: money, verification, executive relationships and sustainable delivery mattered, and a quiet incident year still ended with a missed acquisition objective and exhausted functions. I cannot compare it with the other difficulties because I did not play them."

6. Did the risk list help you decide what to work on — the order, or the words beside each risk?
"Both helped, but the concrete attack paths and business consequences did most of the work. The initial High pipeline and supplier risks stood above Elevated recovery, then supplier and recovery improved while pipeline remained High; those differences were visible and useful. Once the order settled, I stopped rereading the same paragraphs, and the deferred lower rows plus stale reassessment prompts became easy to walk past."

7. Would you play a second year? What would you do differently?
"Yes, as another assistant-operated playthrough, but it would not replace a human session. I would stagger the two large programmes, commission the team review early, reserve money for acquisition due diligence and restore assurance, and fund cloud treatment sooner rather than letting the pipeline risk stay High. I would use more of the otherwise unused weekly attention for verification and risk reassessment, instead of letting every quiet period become a skip."

### Facilitator's one-line summary

The single most important thing this session showed.

The year produced believable strategic trade-offs and a Q3-to-review story that matched, but its automatic assumption falsely turned a conscious, budget-constrained acceptance into reliance on assurance the player never claimed.
